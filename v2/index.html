A MillionThingsMoving

Nothing here is calculated on the CPU. Every particle's position and velocity lives inside a floating-point texture, and the entire simulation advances by drawing two full-screen quads per frame. Forces are real: a spring pulling toward the target shape, a curl-noise flow field, your cursor, and gravity toward a collapsing core.

State as an image.

A 1024×1024 RGBA float texture holds one million positions — one particle per texel. A second holds velocities. Each frame a fragment shader reads both, integrates the forces, and writes to a spare pair of textures. Next frame they swap. This is ping-ponging, and it is how you simulate a million bodies without ever touching JavaScript.

The vertex shader then reads that position texture directly, so geometry never leaves the GPU. The draw call sends one million vertices and zero coordinates.

Curl noise is
why it looks alive.

Straight noise makes particles jitter. Curl noise takes the curl of a noise field, which is divergence-free — the flow can never compress into a point or blow apart. That constraint is what smoke, ink and water all share, and it's why the drift here reads as fluid rather than random.

Hold space and a gravity well opens at the origin. Particles fall in along curved paths because they keep the angular momentum the flow field gave them — nobody scripted the spiral.
